    Introduction

    Protein language models (pLMs) such as the ESM family are masked language models trained on a large corpus of protein sequences. As is standard in BERT style architectures, for each sequence 15% of the residues are masked, and the model is tasked with predicting the masked portion given the unmasked. Note that this is where the model differs from LLMs, which are autoregressive. Here context on either end is critical, since they determine downstream things like structure prediction.

    Impressively, despite only being trained on these sequences, the representations built up by these models perform well on downstream tasks such as structure prediction [Lin23] and function [Che25]. These predictions generalize to unannotated metagenomic sequences [Lin23], such as uncharacterised microbial enzymes and viral sequences [Fla24], [Thu25].

    Understanding the internals of these models has significant biological and biosafety implications. On one hand, internal representations of foundation models have been used to derive novel biological and medical insights [Good25]. Conversely, these tools can be harnessed to malicious ends, by a bad actor or, more likely, given the fact that these foundation models are used as tools by general LLM agents for iterative design, and we move closer to wet lab orchestration of structures synthesized by agents using foundation models.

    The prevalent mitigation strategy is filtering out harmful sequences from the pretraining corpus [Hay25], [Bri26] and evaluating perplexity on those held out sequences after training, in the hopes that the harmful capability has been removed. However, given that these models excel at generalization, it is plausible that the model retains the capability in an underdeveloped circuit (showing low perplexity on the particular held out sequences). Ensuring that the capability is removed on a structural level would significantly enhance biosafety of these deployed models.

    Existing work, including [IPLM], has used SAEs to better understand pLM internals. They are a significant step toward understanding the latent representations of these models and demonstrate that the latent representations encode geometric structure of protein sequences.

    Structural inference

    Recent work [Bak25], [Gor26] has developed susceptibilities as a means of connecting structure in data to structure in the model. Susceptibilities characterize a model's first order response to perturbations in the training distribution (Part 2, Section 3.3, Definition 1). The framework is extremely general and translates immediately to the masked language setting.

    In fact, protein language has far more structure than natural language, making the framework more naturally applicable in this setting.

    Setup

    We will first consider ESM-8M, which has $|\Sigma|=33$ (tokens) with 6 layers and 20 attention heads per layer. This would be a first step, as we expect more interesting results at the 650M scale. Though given findings for ESM-8M in our prior work [Set26] and related SAE work [IPLM], we expect non-trivial structure at the 8M scale.

    The susceptibilities framework translated for masked language models

    The standard learning problem setup $(p,q,\varphi)$ defined earlier (Part 1, Section 2) applies here. Concretely:

    • $X\times Y$ is the training data, where a sample $(x,y)$ consists of a protein sequence $x$ with 15% of its residue positions masked, and $y$ the ground truth in those masked positions;
    • $W$: parameter space (unchanged);
    • $w\mapsto p(\cdot|w)$: the parameter function map (unchanged);
    • $q(x,y)$: the true distribution on $X\times Y$. The change to masked language modelling from autoregressive does not change much here;
    • $\varphi$: prior on $W$ (unchanged).

    Also recall that given a component $C$ of the model and a sample $(x,y)\in X\times Y$, the per sample susceptibility (Part 2, Section 3.6, Definition 4) is

    $$\chi^C_{(x,y)}=-\Cov_\beta\big(\phi_C,\ \lxy(w)-L(w)\big),$$

    where $\beta$ is the inverse temperature used to temper the posterior we sample $w$ from (Part 2, Section 2.3), and

    $$\phi_C(w)=\delta(u-\us)\big[L(w)-L(\ws)\big],$$

    writing $W=U\times C$ (Part 2, Section 3.5, Definition 3).

    None of this is blocked by the change to masked language models.

    Spectrum of protein language

    The key insight of [CM25], exploited in [Bak25] and [Gor26], is that data admits “modes” or patterns that explain why a certain token $y$ follows a context $x$. This is the structure on the data side. Each pair $(x,y)$ can be explained by a (hopefully sparse) set of patterns. A model learns these patterns, and this is reflected in the internal structure of the model. Susceptibilities isolate this structure by probing the model with data and measuring its response to this data.

    Formally, if one had access to the conditional $q(y|x)$, applying SVD to this matrix would yield a change of basis (Part 2, Section 5.3, Eq. (44) for the details) from a per token basis to a modes basis. Each context-token pair $(x,y)$ expressed in the modes basis yields a propensity profile $\{s_{\alpha\beta}(xy)\}_{\alpha\beta}$.

    Intuition$(x,y)$ excites mode $\alpha$; repeated from Part 2, Section 5.4, Intuition 12

    Given a context $x$ and a continuation $y$, we say $(x,y)$ excites mode $\alpha$ if, when we write in the modes basis

    $$y\circ\xh=\sum s_{\alpha\beta}(xy)\,e_{\alpha\beta},$$

    the diagonal term $s_{\alpha\alpha}(xy)$ dominates. This says that:

    1the context $x$ is an $\alpha$ pattern;

    2$y$ is a continuation the $\alpha$ pattern predicts.

    If $x$ looks like an $\alpha$ pattern but $y$ is not a continuation consistent with $\alpha$, we get the off diagonal term $s_{\alpha\beta}(xy)$ being large.

    Note none of this is a statement about the model, this is purely a statement about structure in the data distribution. Susceptibilities connect this structure in data to structure in the model.

    Examplemodes in natural language

    In natural language, examples of modes could be induction patterns, or "." followed by capital letters, among others. See [Gor26] for more examples.

    Cautionthe problem

    We have no access to $q(y|x)$, and consequently no means of getting the modes basis.

    Protein language modes constrained by biophysics and evolution

    Protein language is comprised of a 21-letter alphabet (20 amino acids and a gap) and the primary object of study is the protein sequence of length $L$ over this alphabet. A key problem in biology is predicting structure (the 3D folded protein) and function from this sequence. Seminal work [Anf73] provides strong evidence that amino acid sequences strongly determine folded structure. Combined with the fact that naturally occurring proteins stem from an evolutionary process, there are many constraints on protein sequences.

    Evolution and multiple sequence alignment

    The first characterization of a protein sequence is evolutionary. Given a sequence $x$, one can query large sequence databases such as UniRef [UniRef] for homologs, i.e. other sequences that share a common ancestor evolutionarily. For example, hemoglobin proteins are found across species and are evolutionarily closely related but distinct.

    Given a collection of evolutionarily related sequences $\{x^i\}_i$ (called a family), one can perform multiple sequence alignment (MSA) on these sequences.

    MSA inserts gaps so each column corresponds to the same structural position across sequences. Across a family $\{x^i\}_i$, evolution leads to discrepancies, so relating raw residue positions across sequences does not make sense. MSA aligns them so that they do match up.

    MSA aligned families are the input to systems such as AlphaFold [AF2], which can predict structure with staggering accuracy given MSAs. The biological intuition behind this is that these families share folding properties, and MSA is a means of sampling from this fold consistent family.

    Structural constraints

    Let $x_i$ and $x_j$ be residue positions. Suppose that upon folding $x_i$ and $x_j$ are spatially close in 3D space, then the amino acids at these positions likely covary [Göb94]. For example, if $x_i$ flips from positive to negative charge and $x_j$ is spatially close to $x_i$, then it must mutate accordingly.

    Remarkcorrelation isn't the whole story

    Correlation isn't the whole story, since

    $$i\text{ and }j\text{ covary}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{close in the folded 3D space}$$

    is false. In particular transitivity fails:

    $$(i\leftrightarrow j\text{ covary})\ \wedge\ (j\leftrightarrow k\text{ covary})\ \Longrightarrow\ (i\leftrightarrow k\text{ covary}),$$

    but $i$ and $k$ might not be close in the folded structure. More sophisticated techniques like the Potts model [Mor11] account for this.

    What the extra structure buys us

    These patterns provide a large corpus of possible modes in the data, each in a biologically motivated manner. This extra structure leads to the following improvements over the LLM setting.

    1Compute a susceptibility matrix using per token susceptibilities as in [Gor26] and cluster as described there (Part 2, Section 6.2). A given cluster corresponds to a particular $\alpha$–$\beta$ mode. Concretely, for a given cluster we have pairs $(x,y)$ where $x$ is a masked sequence and $y$ is the amino acid in the masked positions. We can ascribe meaning to the cluster by querying the corpus described above () for matches. This would be a biologically motivated means of annotation.

    2For a given pattern in the corpus above, compute per pattern susceptibilities as described in Appendix B.4 of [Gor26], following [Emb]. Concretely,

    $$\hat\chi(P)=\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{(x,y)\in P}\chi_{xy},$$

    where $P$ is formed by sampling $(x,y)$ from the corpus above.

    Experiments

    Experiment 1

    We first consider ESM-8M.

    1Draw 50k protein sequences from UniRef50, by first clustering sequences that match up to 80% identity and sampling 1 sequence per cluster (same procedure as detailed in [IPLM] and [Set26]). Then generate samples $(x,y)$ by masking a single position for each sequence to get a pair $(x,y)$. Compute

    $$\chi^{(\ell)}_{ij}=\chi^{C_j}_{(x_i,y_i)},\qquad \ell\in\{1,\dots,6\}\ \text{(layers)},\quad j\in\{1,\dots,20\}\ \text{(attention heads)} .$$

    2Cluster using the same procedure as [Gor26].

    3For each cluster $i$, assemble pairs $(x,y)$ in the cluster and search the corpus above () for matching patterns.

    Safety implications

    A more robust mitigation strategy using susceptibilities

    1Most direct. Define patterns from the filtered set (or a proxy for this) and compute $\hat\chi(P)$. High susceptibility indicates the model recognizes this pattern and warrants further investigation.

    Remark$\hat\chi(P)$ can be high for innocuous reasons

    $\hat\chi(P)$ might be high for innocuous reasons: harmful viral proteins and innocent proteins might both have things like $\alpha$-helices.

    2To control for we need more refined tests. Concretely, a model that has learned underlying generic machinery like $\alpha$-helix, $\beta$-sheet and other patterns would still respond to harmful proteins, as they contain these same patterns. We need to check for dedicated structure, i.e. for a harmful protein sequence $(x,y)$ we have the propensity profile $\{s_{\alpha\beta}(xy)\}_{\alpha\beta}$. A model will still respond to the modes that are generic capabilities but fail to respond to more specific modes. So, if we assume (and this is a strong assumption, though hopefully mitigated by sparsity of propensity profiles, Part 2, Section 5.5, Remark 8) that generic capability patterns are enumerated by the patterns in the corpus, we can regress

    $$\hat\chi(P_{\text{harmful}})\approx\sum_k c_k\,\hat\chi(P_k)$$

    and look at the residual. If the harmful capability has been removed, then $\hat\chi(P_{\text{harmful}})$ lies in the span of the generic modes. Else, it is in a component orthogonal to that span, and the residual tells us which component is responsible.

    Even if our corpus is incomplete, running the regression gives us a better idea of where the capability is hiding. We can look at the complement.

    3More refined tests. We can refine further in multiple ways. For example, if we know a harmful protein has a position with a buried helix, we can compare it with benign ones with buried helices.

    Conductance tests

    Fine-tune a model on harmful material and measure how the clusters move. This is directly analogous to Section 4.5 of [Gor26], where they define clusters in Pythia-14M and measure their conductance under the susceptibility geometry of models up to 1.4B. Here we define clusters in the filtered model and measure their conductance under the fine-tuned model's geometry, asking whether it stays below what we would expect for a random set of probes of the same size.

    Developmental interpretability

    We can checkpoint across training or finetuning and track if and when capabilities emerge, following the framework outlined in [Emb].

    Practical value: monitor during training for emergence of dangerous capabilities, rather than a post-hoc check.

    Extensions

    [WM26] introduce patterning, which inverts the structural inference framework to find data interventions to produce desired structural changes in model internals, e.g. suppression of harmful capabilities.

    Compute estimates

    This is a rough compute estimate that I asked Claude to estimate using the figures in Gordon 26 and Setlur 26.

    Two things anchor these numbers. [Gor26, Appendix A.2] gives the SGLD settings its susceptibility runs actually used, and [Set26, Appendix A.3] gives measured A100 times for putting ESM-2 8M over exactly the kind of corpus Experiment 1 needs. Everything below is quoted in hours on a single A100-SXM4-80GB, the card used in [Set26].

    Where the compute goes

    The susceptibility matrix of costs three things.

    1Sampling. Run pSGLD to get draws of $w$ from the tempered posterior. [Gor26, Table 5] uses, for Pythia-14M, 4 chains, 100 draws per chain, 55 steps between draws, gradient batch 16, $\eps=10^{-5}$, $\gamma=300$ and $n\beta=3$. That is 5,500 steps per chain, 22,000 steps in all, and 400 draws of $w$.

    2Per sample losses. For every retained draw $w_t$, evaluate $\lxy(w_t)$ on every pair. This is the term that dominates, and what it costs depends on how many pairs one forward pass yields.

    3Observables. For every draw and every component $C$, evaluate $\phi_C$ of on a fixed evaluation batch. ESM-8M has $6\times20=120$ attention heads, against 32 components for Pythia-14M.

    How many pairs 50,000 proteins give

    The 780,000 of [Gor26] is 13 Pile subsets at 60,000 sequences each, one pair per sequence, so their row count carries no multiplier over positions. But [Bak25, Appendix C.4] does take the multiplier: 160 contexts at 1,023 positions gives 163,680 samples from 160 forward passes, because causal masking hands you the loss at every position of a sequence in one pass.

    The analogue here is to move the mask. For a sequence $x$, mask position $i$ and we have one pair; mask a different position of the same $x$ and we have a different sample; and so on. Taking about 20 choices of masked residue for each of the 50,000 sequences gives on the order of $10^6$ pairs, the same order as [Gor26]. The ceiling is the $50{,}000\times240\approx1.2\times10^7$ maskable positions in the corpus.

    A masked model does not get these for free the way a causal one does. Moving the mask changes $x$, so $N$ pairs costs $N$ forward passes and term ② is linear in the number of pairs.

    The unit cost is one forward pass. ESM-2 8M has about $7.4\times10^6$ non-embedding parameters, so roughly $1.5\times10^7$ FLOPs per residue and about $4\times10^9$ FLOPs for a 240 residue pass (the corpus averages 240 residues, from Table 6 of [Set26], whose corpus is the same size). A million pairs at 400 draws is then about $1.6\times10^{18}$ FLOPs.

    The draws are shared across components. The expectation $\langle-\rangle_\beta$ is over the full tempered posterior, and a component enters only through the observable, so one campaign of 400 draws serves all 120 heads: $\phi_C(w_t)$ is obtained by splicing $C$'s coordinates from $w_t$ into $\ws$ and evaluating $L$ there. Nothing in the pipeline has to be repeated per head.

    Two published numbers, and what they say

    1[Bak25, Appendix C.4] computed per-token susceptibilities for a 3M parameter model on a single TPU v4 in 3 hours. That job is not comparable in the term that matters: their 163,680 samples come from only 160 contexts, so their term ② is 160 forward passes per draw against our $10^6$. Their 3 hours is essentially term ①, and it confirms only that the sampling is cheap.

    2[Gor26, Appendix A.3] ran Pythia-14M on 4 nodes of 4 H200s each, within 5 days of wall-clock, and states that exact GPU-hours were not logged. That is a ceiling of about 1,920 H200-hours. Scaling it here: about $3\times$ for an A100 against an H200, about $\tfrac14$ for our 240 residue sequences against their context, about $1.3\times$ for our larger pair count, and roughly parity per token, since Pythia-14M's body is only 1.2M parameters but its 50,304 token output projection dominates it, where ESM-2 8M's body is 7.4M with a 33 token head. That comes to roughly 1,900 A100-hours, an order of magnitude above .

    Cautionwhich of the two to believe

    The same FLOP count applied to their own run gives about 13 H200-hours for term ②, so their ceiling stands at least a hundred times above their own arithmetic. It is five days times every card allocated, for a run that also covers the clustering, the UMAP, the SAE comparison and the $n\beta=0$ rerun, and the paper says the hours were never logged. So it is not evidence that the arithmetic is wrong.

    But it is the only published number for a comparable job, and the arithmetic's weakest input is the assumed 10–20% utilisation. A model of width 320 on short sequences can easily sit at 5%, which triples on its own. Budget as though the larger figure might be real.

    What to ask for

    Bottom up, the 8M programme is 120–240 A100-hours for one clean pass of Experiments 1 and 2, the conductance test and a ten checkpoint developmental run. That prices a single run with the SGLD settings of [Gor26] transplanted unchanged, and $\eps$, $\gamma$ and $n\beta$ will need tuning for ESM, which is the first thing to do and the thing most likely to be repeated. At three to five rounds that is 400–1,200. Top down from it is around 1,900.

    So 1,000 A100-hours is the optimistic case rather than a safe ask. Ask for 2,000 for the 8M programme, which covers the top down figure with a little room, or 2,500 if a 650M run is in scope from the start. A developmental sweep at 650M is 2,500–4,500 on its own and is better as a second request, once the 8M numbers say what the utilisation really is.

    Remarkwhat is assumed rather than read off

    The SGLD settings, the component counts, the corpus size and the A100 times are all taken from the two papers. Assumed here: that ESM-8M holds 10–20% of the card's bf16 peak, which is where a 7M parameter model on 240 token sequences tends to sit; that the SGLD settings of Pythia-14M carry over unchanged, which is the thing to tune first; that the evaluation batch for $L$ is 50k residues; and that the corpus annotation scales from the CPU hours in [Set26].

    A learning problem is a triple $(p,q,\varphi)$: $p$ a model, a distribution on $X$ for each parameter setting; $q$ what we want to learn; $\varphi$ a prior, our best guess as to which $w\in W$ gives us the lowest loss.

    The susceptibility of the observable $\phi$ to the perturbation $q_h=(1-h)q+hq'$ is the first order response

    $$\chi=\frac{1}{n\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial h}\langle\phi\rangle_{\beta,h}\Big|_{h=0} .$$

    The per-token susceptibility of $C$ for $(x,y)\in X\times Y$ is

    $$\chi^C_{(x,y)}=-\Cov_\beta\big(\phi_C,\ \lxy(w)-L(w)\big),$$

    where $\lxy(w)=-\log p(y|x,w)$.

    The observable attached to a component $C$, writing $W=U\times C$, is

    $$\phi_C(w)=\delta(u-\us)\big[L(w)-L(\ws)\big],$$

    the population loss restricted to the slice where only the weights of $C$ move.

    $\beta$ is the inverse temperature of the tempered posterior we sample from; the LLC estimator uses $\beta=1/\log n$.

    $$\Cc=USV^\top=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lp}\tilde s_\alpha\,u_\alpha\circ\vh_\alpha$$

    SVD of the conditional $\Cc$, expressed in the token basis: $u_\alpha$ are the continuation patterns and $\vh_\alpha$ the context patterns.

    $$e_{\alpha\beta}(x)(y)=\vh_\alpha(x)\,\langle u_\beta,y\rangle$$

    This is called the modes basis.

    Propensities are the change of basis coefficients between the token and modes basis:

    $$s_{\alpha\beta}(xy):=\big\langle y\circ\xh,\ e_{\alpha\beta}\big\rangle_\Hil=e_{\alpha\beta}(x)(y).$$

    We say $(x,y)$ excites mode $\alpha$ if the diagonal term $s_{\alpha\alpha}(xy)$ dominates: the context $x$ is an $\alpha$ pattern, and $y$ is a continuation the $\alpha$ pattern predicts.

    The decomposition is useful when the propensity profile of a sample $(x,y)$ is sparse: $x$ aligns with only a few context patterns, $y$ with a few continuation patterns, and the diagonal term dominates.

    They build $X_{ij}=\hat\chi^{C_j}_{(x_i,y_i)}$ over 780,000 samples and 32 attention heads, and cluster the rows of $X$. Samples whose propensity profiles agree elicit similar first order responses, so they land in the same cluster.
